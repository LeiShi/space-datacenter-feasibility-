First-principles assessment · October 2026

Can AI data centers move to orbit?

Physically, yes. Economically, not on any credible timeline. A model with one kilowatt of compute as the unit shows why: the sunlight is free, but the chips are two thirds of the bill, and orbit cannot make them cheaper.

PhysicsFeasible. 96% sunlight at 650 km and a radiator only 28% the size of the solar array.
ReliabilityInference is fine. Large synchronous training breaks without new fault-tolerant software.
EconomicsAbout 2× the ground cost around 2030. Parity needs $200/kg launch and satellites 2–5× cheaper per kg than Starlink.

03 · Method

One kilowatt as the unit

Every quantity is normalised to 1 kW of IT power: electricity reaching accelerators, hosts, memory and network. A data center is N copies of that kilowatt. Scale effects (launch cadence, debris, communications) are treated separately.

  • Orbit. Beta angle for each day of the year from J2 nodal precession, giving the eclipse fraction. Earth infrared and albedo loads on a sun-edge-on radiator from a 300,000-point view-factor integral over a Lambertian Earth.
  • Power and heat. Array sized for IT plus bus at end of life; all electrical power becomes heat that the radiator must reject.
  • Reliability. Radiation event rates from Google's TPU beam test, scaled by orbital dose; Young–Daly checkpointing; failures never repaired.
  • Economics. Levelised cost per delivered kW-year. The ground facility lasts 15 years and gets refilled with chips; a satellite's power and thermal systems die with its chips.

04 · Power

The one thing space does better

A dawn–dusk orbit rides the terminator, so sunlight is nearly continuous. Computing the beta angle day by day, eclipses only occur in a season around one solstice, and they are short.

AltitudeTime in eclipseLongest eclipseEclipse season

Per square metre of panel that is about 6× the yearly energy of a good ground site (1361 × 0.96 W/m² against 1000 W/m² × 22% capacity factor), consistent with Google's “up to 8×”.

Cell choice decides the economics. Triple-junction III-V cells are 30–33% efficient but cost $100–450 per watt; at 1.3 kW of array per kW of compute that is 4–20× the price of the GPUs. At scale the only option is silicon, as on Starlink: about $0.5/W, 22% efficient, 235 W/m² at the start of life and 207 W/m² after five years of 2.5%/yr fade.

Counting only the array and its share of structure, electricity from orbit costs about $1.80/kWh today, $0.34 around 2030, $0.08 around 2035, and $0.014 under advocate assumptions, against about $0.05 for ground solar. The 6× sunlight advantage is consumed by launch and space hardware until launch reaches about $200/kg.

05 · Cooling

Harder than on Earth, but not the show-stopper

In a dawn–dusk orbit the arrays face the Sun and the radiators can stay edge-on to it. What remains is Earth's infrared glow and reflected sunlight. With one face toward the zenith and one toward Earth, the integrated view factors are 0 and 0.81, and the effective sink temperature is about 205 K.

At 320 K the panel rejects about 740 W/m². The array delivers 207 W/m², so the radiator needs only ~28% of the array's area. The problem is mass: pumped-loop radiators with headers and micrometeoroid armour weigh 6–15 kg/m² (the ISS system is ~15). At 6 kg/m² the radiator is the heaviest subsystem.

Temperature is the master variable. GPUs want ~45 °C coolant and throttle near 85 °C junction, which limits the radiator to about 305–335 K. Chips qualified for hotter operation would be the single most valuable change for an orbital design: at 360 K the same panel rejects ~1,270 W/m².

Two public claims, checked. Starcloud's whitepaper uses 838 W/m² at 20 °C, which is exactly an ideal black body with no Earth load; the model gives 475–536 W/m². SpaceX's AI1 implies ~1,360 W/m² of planform, which needs a ~362 K (89 °C) radiator mean once Earth's load is included, hotter than any GPU liquid loop runs today.

Does a heat pump help? Raising the radiator temperature shrinks it as T4, but the pump's work must be generated and radiated too. Expanding to first order, a small lift pays only when (4ηc − 1) × (radiator kg per W rejected) exceeds the array's kg per W generated. At 50% of Carnot it saves 5–10% with heavy ISS-class radiators and nothing with light deployables.

06 · Radiation & computation errors

Fine for inference, hard for synchronous training

The dose has three parts: trapped protons in the South Atlantic Anomaly (hard to shield, rising steeply with altitude), outer-belt electrons at high latitude (stopped by a few mm of aluminium), and cosmic rays and solar particles over the poles (single-event effects rather than dose). The curve below is a two-component fit to published anchors, good to about a factor of three.

The first ~5 mm of aluminium removes the electrons; after that protons dominate and extra shielding buys little. Dose rises about e-fold per 120 km above ~600 km, which caps commercial HBM near 700 km. Ten millimetres of aluminium is 27 kg/m², affordable around compact compute boards (~1–2 kg/kW) but not around arrays or radiators.

The only public data on a modern datacenter accelerator is Google's 67 MeV proton test of the Trillium TPU with HBM: one silent data corruption (SDC) per ~17 rad, one uncorrectable HBM error (UECC) per ~44 rad, one functional interrupt per ~5 krad, HBM irregularities from ~2 krad, and no hard failure to 15 krad. At 650 km behind 10 mm, a 16,384-chip job sees an SDC every 3.6 minutes and an HBM UECC every 9 minutes. If each UECC crashes the job, optimal checkpointing leaves ~10% goodput, against ~90% on the ground.

Inference tolerates this: about one corrupted answer per 10 million. Training needs recoverable memory errors, checksummed matrix multiplies and asynchronous, low-communication methods. None is how frontier models are trained today. No heavy-ion latchup data exist yet for any datacenter GPU or TPU.

No repair. Failed GPUs stay failed. At 3–6% per year, the fleet averages 86–90% of launched capacity over five years. Flying spares costs exactly the IT dollars they protect.

The link physics explains Google's 100–200 m spacing. A 10 cm aperture at 1.55 µm has a near field out to ~6.5 km; inside 1 km it catches essentially all the light, at 100 km 0.26%, at 5,000 km one part in a million. Commercial DWDM receivers need hundreds of microwatts, so terabit links force satellites to fly almost touching, which trades a bandwidth problem for a collision problem.

09 · Economics

Chips are two thirds of the bill, and orbit cannot touch them

On the ground a delivered kW-year costs about $11.6k: 66% compute hardware, 15% facility, 7% electricity, 12% operations. That is $2.37 per GPU-hour, matching SemiAnalysis's independent 2026 figure. Orbit removes electricity, the building and most operations, then adds qualification cost, eclipse throttling, unrepaired failures, and a “facility” thrown away with each chip generation.

With free launch and a free satellite, orbit would be only 6–20% cheaper at today's chip prices. That is the ceiling.

Today~2030~2035Advocate

The moving threshold. If grid scarcity pushes ground power prices up, orbit breaks even when delivered power exceeds about $0.84/kWh (2030 design, $200/kg launch) or $0.24/kWh (2035 design). Behind-the-meter gas and solar-plus-storage supply data centers at roughly $0.08–0.15/kWh, which caps how far scarcity can help.

The stronger form of the argument is time-to-power: a $30k/kW chip idle for a year costs ~$7.6k/kW in capital, equivalent to ~$0.87/kWh. Orbit wins that race only if satellites and launches can be produced faster than ground power.

Varying 13 inputs across a generous 2030s envelope (launch $100–3,000/kg, spacecraft hardware $300–3,000/kg, electricity $0.05–0.15/kWh, and more), the ratio has a median of 2.4× and a 90% interval of 1.4–4.7×. Fewer than 0.1% of 20,000 samples favour orbit. Launch price and spacecraft cost per kilogram dominate; electricity price barely registers.

Chip price shifts the balance. As chips get cheaper per watt, the ratio tends toward the ratio of infrastructure costs. At $10k/kW, the 2030 case worsens to 2.8× while the advocate case improves to 0.60×. The advocates' story holds together only if space infrastructure becomes much cheaper than ground infrastructure and chips get cheap.

10 · Scale

A gigawatt is hundreds of heavy launches, every five years

Design1 GW mass100-t launchesArray + radiatorRe-entry

The whole world launched a few thousand tonnes to orbit in 2025. SpaceX's “100 GW per year” would need about 9,300 Starship flights a year at advocate mass, or 30,000 at the 2030 mass. Area is not the constraint: a gigawatt needs 6–7.5 km² of thin structure.

What would change the verdict

  1. A compute satellite of 100 kW or more running a year, with published error, thermal and failure statistics.
  2. Starship commercial prices below $300/kg.
  3. A satellite power-and-thermal system sold below ~$300/kg at more than 60 W/kg.
  4. Heavy-ion latchup data and in-orbit SDC rates for a datacenter accelerator.
  5. A frontier-scale training run done with asynchronous, fault-tolerant methods.
